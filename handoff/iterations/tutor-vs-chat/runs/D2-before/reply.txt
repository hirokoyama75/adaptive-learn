Your final set is right, but the justification is not. The zero on the right is irrelevant; what matters is what you're dividing **by**.

Dividing both sides by \(x\) is allowed only if \(x \neq 0\). It doesn't matter whether the other side is 0, 6, or anything else. So the division doesn't prove \(x=2\). It proves only:

> **If** \(x \neq 0\), **then** \(x=2\).

You still owe the case \(x=0\): substituting gives \(0(0-2)=0\), so \(x=0\) is a solution. Combine the two cases and you get \(\{0,2\}\).

Notice that your own observation is the counterexample to your rule. You found a solution where \(x=0\), which is exactly the value you divided by. If the division were "always legal here," that solution would have been impossible; instead it was nearly lost and you recovered it by checking.

A reliable habit for this shape:

\[
x(x-2)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=0 \ \text{or}\ x-2=0
\]

That's the zero-product property: a product is zero only when at least one factor is zero. If you do divide, write the restriction explicitly ("for \(x\neq0\)") and check the excluded value separately.
