**Check:** 3x + 6 = 18. Divide both sides by 3 → x + 2 = 6 → x = 4. Substituting back: 3(4) + 6 = 12 + 6 = 18. ✓ The worksheet's route gives 3x = 12 → x = 4, same answer.

**Why both work:** the rule "multiply/divide before add/subtract" tells you how to *evaluate* an expression. Solving isn't evaluating — you may undo operations in any order, as long as you do the same thing to the *entire* both sides. Think of it as peeling wrapping off a box: outside layer first or innermost first both get you to the contents.

**One condition on your method:** dividing first is clean only when the divisor goes into *every* term. Here 3 divides 3x, 6, and 18, so nothing is left behind. If it were 3x + 7 = 18, dividing by 3 gives x + 7/3 = 6 — still correct, just uglier, and the fractions are where people slip. That's the real failure mode, not the order itself.

The genuinely invalid moves are dividing only one side, or only some terms on a side.

Worksheets usually teach subtract-first because it keeps everything in whole numbers and still works when the coefficient doesn't divide the other terms. Follow it if a later section builds on that pattern; otherwise your route is fine.

Quick habit for the rest of the set: after each solve, substitute your x back in. That catches order-independent slips in about five seconds.
